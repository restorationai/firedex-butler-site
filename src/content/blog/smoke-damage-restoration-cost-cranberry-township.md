If a stovetop fire, a furnace puffback, or a small electrical fire filled your Cranberry Township home with smoke but the flames never reached the structure, smoke-only restoration typically costs $500 to $10,000. Where you land in that range depends mostly on how far the soot traveled, what kind of soot it is, and whether your HVAC system carried it through the whole house. A single room with light, dry soot sits near the bottom. A whole-house odor problem with contaminated ductwork and a full contents cleanout sits near the top.

This is different from a structural fire loss, where framing, roofing, or drywall actually burned. If flames damaged the structure itself, the numbers run higher. [Fire Damage Restoration Cost in Butler, PA](/blog/fire-damage-restoration-cost-butler-pa/) covers what homeowners pay when there's structural rebuild involved. This guide is for the more common case: the fire was small or contained, but smoke, soot, and odor spread through the house anyway.

## How is smoke-only damage different from fire damage, cost-wise?

Smoke-only restoration costs less than structural fire restoration because there's no framing, drywall, or roofing to rebuild. The work is cleaning, odor treatment, and sometimes repainting rather than demolition and reconstruction.

A kitchen fire that's extinguished quickly, a furnace puffback that coats a basement in soot, or a space heater fire that's caught early are typical smoke-only scenarios. The fire department may have been there, and the damage can look alarming (soot on every surface, a smell that won't quit), but the structural bones of the house are intact. That keeps the job in cleaning-and-restoration territory instead of general-contracting territory. If you're not sure which category your loss falls into, [what to do after a house fire](/blog/what-to-do-after-a-house-fire-cranberry-township/) walks through the first 24 hours and how to tell the difference.

## What actually moves the price up or down?

The biggest cost driver is soot type, not square footage alone. Dry soot (from paper, wood, or a fast-burning fire) wipes off relatively easily with dry chemical sponges. Wet or protein soot (from a grease fire or burning plastic) is sticky, smears if wiped wrong, and often needs solvent-based cleaners or full surface replacement.

Square footage affected matters too, but it's secondary. A 200-square-foot kitchen with oily soot baked into cabinet finishes can cost more to clean than a 600-square-foot basement with light, dry residue.

A few other factors that change the number:

- **HVAC and duct contamination.** Smoke drawn into return vents spreads soot and odor through the whole house via the duct system. If the furnace was running when the fire happened, assume the ducts need cleaning, not just the rooms you can see.
- **Contents cleaning.** Furniture, electronics, clothing, and personal items absorb smoke odor even when they look undamaged. Soft contents (fabric, upholstery) usually need ozone or thermal chamber treatment; hard contents get wiped and sealed.
- **Odor treatment method.** Thermal fogging and hydroxyl generators cost more than a simple wipe-down but are necessary when odor has soaked into porous materials like drywall and subfloor.
- **Whether drywall or insulation has to come out.** Smoke odor that's penetrated insulation inside a wall cavity usually can't be cleaned out; the insulation gets replaced. That adds demolition and drywall costs even on a smoke-only job.

## Do smoke-damaged walls need to be repainted, or just cleaned?

It depends on the soot type and how long it sat. Light dry soot on painted drywall often cleans up with dry sponging and a degreasing wash, with no repaint needed. Oily or protein soot, or soot that sat for more than a few days, usually stains the paint permanently and needs a stain-blocking primer and repaint to fully seal in the odor.

As a rule of thumb: if the surface still smells like smoke after a proper cleaning and the odor doesn't come back after airing out the room, cleaning was enough. If the smell lingers or the wall shows a yellow-brown cast after cleaning, sealing and repainting is the only way to stop the odor from re-emerging as the weather warms and the walls off-gas.

## Will homeowners insurance cover smoke damage cleanup?

Most homeowners policies cover smoke damage cleanup when it results from a sudden, accidental fire, the same way they cover the fire itself. Fire is a named peril on nearly every standard policy, and that typically extends to the smoke and soot the fire produced, not just the burned structure. Check your specific policy language and deductible, since coverage details vary by insurer, as explained by the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance).

What insurance usually does not cover well is smoke odor with no identifiable sudden event behind it, like chronic cigarette smoke or a chimney that's been smoking slowly for years. Document the fire with photos, keep the fire department report if one was filed, and get a written restoration scope before cleaning begins. That paper trail is what protects the claim.

## What happens during a professional smoke damage cleanup?

A smoke-only job generally runs through five phases:

1. **Assessment.** A technician walks the property, identifies the soot type (dry, wet/protein, or oily), checks HVAC contamination, and documents everything with photos before touching anything.
2. **Containment and ventilation.** HEPA air scrubbers run to pull soot particulate and odor out of the air while the rest of the cleanup happens, keeping residue from spreading to unaffected rooms.
3. **Surface cleaning.** Dry chemical sponges for light soot, wet cleaning and degreasers for heavier or oily residue, working from ceiling down to floor so cleaned surfaces don't get recontaminated.
4. **Contents cleaning.** Soft goods go through ozone or thermal chambers; hard goods get wiped, degreased, and sealed on-site or at a cleaning facility.
5. **Odor treatment and final seal.** Thermal fogging, hydroxyl generators, or ozone treatment neutralizes residual odor, with a stain-blocking primer and repaint where the soot has penetrated the paint film.

Duct cleaning happens alongside steps 2 through 4 whenever the HVAC system ran during or after the fire.
