## Is the water coming from rain, a floor drain backup, or something else?

The source determines how the water is classified and handled, and it's rarely obvious just by looking at it. Rainwater that runs in under a garage door typically starts as [Category 1](https://iicrc.org/s500/) clean water under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), but wind-driven storm rain and surface water pooling in off the driveway are treated as Category 3, grossly contaminated water, because of what they pick up off pavement and soil on the way in.

A garage floor drain that backs up instead of draining is a different animal. If it's tied to the sanitary sewer line, anything coming up through it is Category 3 black water, full stop, and that changes what can be dried versus what has to be removed. Cranberry's heavy clay soil also pushes hydrostatic groundwater through slab cracks during wet stretches, which shows up as damp concrete rather than standing water but still needs a moisture reading before you store anything back on that floor.

## Does homeowners insurance cover a flooded garage?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a burst supply line to a garage sink or a water heater located in the garage, but they exclude gradual seepage and typically exclude sewer or floor-drain backups unless you've added a water backup endorsement. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) lays out this split clearly, and it's the single most common surprise homeowners run into after a garage flood.

If your garage flooded because storm water flowed in off the driveway or yard, that's generally treated as surface flooding, which standard homeowners policies don't cover at all; it needs a separate flood policy through the [NFIP](https://www.floodsmart.gov/). Document the source with photos and a timestamp before anything is moved or dried, since the adjuster will ask which scenario applies before approving the claim.

## What happens to stored items and drywall in a wet garage?

Drywall that's wicked water more than a few inches up the wall usually gets cut out rather than dried in place, because the paper facing holds moisture and feeds mold growth that can start within 24 to 48 hours on a wet surface, per [EPA guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home). Stored holiday boxes, tools, and cardboard act like sponges and are often the first thing to grow visible mold, sometimes before the drywall even shows a stain.

Anything salvageable, totes, tools, furniture staged in the garage during a move, benefits from being pulled out, inventoried, and dried or cleaned off-site rather than left in a damp space while the structure dries. ## How do you stop a garage flood from happening again?

Most repeat garage flooding in Cranberry Township traces back to grading that slopes toward the garage instead of away from it, a downspout dumping at the foundation, or a floor drain tied into an undersized line. A grading or drainage contractor, not a restoration company, is who fixes the slope, extends the downspout, or resets the apron so water sheds away from the door threshold.

A restoration crew can tell you where the water entered and how far it traveled, which is useful information to hand a drainage contractor, but regrading, French drains, and driveway work sit outside a restoration scope.
