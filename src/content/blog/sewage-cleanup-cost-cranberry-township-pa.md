If you came downstairs to a wet floor drain and the smell told you before your eyes did, you're probably trying to figure out two things fast: how bad is this going to get, and what's it going to cost. Cranberry Township's mix of older Fernway-era plumbing and newer finished basements across Marshall Heights and Fox Run means both small lateral backups and full basement losses show up here regularly, and the price gap between the two is enormous.

## How much does sewage cleanup cost in Cranberry Township?

Industry-wide, a contained sewage backup in a small, unfinished area typically costs somewhere between a few hundred and a couple thousand dollars to clean, disinfect and dry. A finished basement with carpet, drywall, and framing exposed to raw sewage for more than a few hours can run well into the thousands once tear-out, antimicrobial treatment, and rebuild are added in.

The range is wide because sewage cleanup isn't one job, it's several possible jobs stacked together: extraction, demolition of contaminated materials, disinfection, drying, odor treatment, and reconstruction. A homeowner who catches a backup within the hour and has an unfinished concrete floor pays for maybe two of those steps. A homeowner who was out of town for a long weekend while a finished rec room soaked pays for all six.

Sewage backup is classified as Category 3 water under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 Standard for Professional Water Damage Restoration](https://iicrc.org/s500/), meaning it's treated as grossly contaminated regardless of how clear the water looks. That classification, not the square footage alone, drives most of the decisions that drive the cost. ## What actually drives the price up or down?

The price moves with how much porous material has to leave the house and how long the sewage had to work on it, not just how many square feet got wet.

| Price driver | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Square footage affected | More area means more extraction, more antimicrobial application, more drying equipment running |
| How long the sewage sat (dwell time) | Contamination and odor compound the longer Category 3 water sits against a material before anyone arrives |
| Which materials are porous | Carpet, pad, and drywall below the waterline are generally not salvageable after a Category 3 loss and have to be removed, not just dried |
| Finished vs. unfinished basement | A finished space with flooring, baseboard, and framed walls costs more to demo and more to rebuild than bare concrete and open studs |
| Odor treatment | Lingering sewage odor often needs more than surface cleaning, which adds a step and a cost line |
| Rebuild after tear-out | Drywall, trim, flooring, and paint to put the room back together is a separate phase from the cleanup itself |

A single unfinished utility room with a floor drain backup might only need extraction, disinfection, and drying. A finished basement in a Fox Run two-story with carpet, drywall, and a wet bar can mean several rooms of demolition before drying equipment even goes in, which is a different cost category entirely.

## Does homeowners insurance cover sewage backup cleanup?

Usually not, unless the policy has a water backup or sewer backup endorsement added. Standard homeowners policies typically exclude damage from a sewer or drain backing up, the same way they exclude flood, and that coverage has to be purchased separately as a rider.

This trips up a lot of homeowners because a burst supply line inside the house is often covered as sudden, accidental water damage, while a sewer backup from the same basement is treated completely differently by the same policy. The [Insurance Information Institute's guide to what standard homeowners insurance covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) is a useful plain-English starting point, but the only way to know for certain is to pull your own declarations page and look for a water backup or sewer/drain backup endorsement by name. If it's not listed, call your agent before you assume anything.

Even with the endorsement, most policies cap sewer backup coverage at a flat dollar amount rather than covering the full loss, so document everything with photos before cleanup starts and keep every receipt.

## Who pays when a Cranberry Township sewer main backs up?

That depends on where the blockage sits. If the clog is in the public sanitary main, the Township's public works crews check and clear it at no charge. If the blockage is in your private lateral, the line running from your house to the main, that repair is the homeowner's responsibility and typically requires a licensed plumber, not a restoration company.

This matters because the two jobs are separate. A restoration company handles the cleanup, disinfection, and rebuild inside your home; clearing or repairing the actual sewer line is plumbing work. In older sections of the township with original cast-iron laterals, like parts of Fernway, a cracked or root-infiltrated private line is a common repeat offender, and no amount of basement cleanup fixes the line itself.

## What should you do first if sewage backs up in your basement?

Shut off water use in the house, keep people and pets out of the affected area, and avoid walking contaminated water through the rest of the home on your shoes. Category 3 water isn't something to mop up with a household vacuum or wet-dry shop vac meant for clean water.

If the backup is a small, contained puddle around a floor drain and you can keep it from spreading, some homeowners handle that themselves with gloves and a disinfectant rated for sewage. Once carpet, drywall, or a finished floor has been sitting in it, or you're not sure how long it's been there, it's a job for a crew equipped for Category 3 contamination.
