If a summer thunderstorm just dropped a limb through your roof off the Route 19/228 corridor, or wind-driven rain found its way into a Fernway attic through a lifted shingle, you're probably staring at two numbers right now: what the repair will cost, and what your insurer will actually pay. Both depend less on the storm itself and more on how fast the breach gets closed and how much water got inside before it did.

## What Does Storm Damage Restoration Cost in Cranberry Township?

Most storm damage jobs in Cranberry Township total $5,000 to $35,000 or more, because a single storm event usually triggers several separate cost phases rather than one flat repair.

Here's how those phases typically break down for Butler County homes:

| Storm Damage Phase | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Emergency tarping / board-up | $300 - $1,500 |
| Water extraction & structural drying | $1,500 - $5,500 |

These are typical industry ranges, not quotes. A cracked soffit vent costs nothing like a tree through a roof deck over a finished Ehrman Farms basement. Any reputable contractor should give you a written scope before work starts rather than a number over the phone.

## What Drives the Price Up or Down?

The single biggest cost driver is how much water got inside before the opening was closed, not the size of the hole itself.

A few factors move the number more than homeowners expect:

- **Response speed.** A hole tarped within hours stays a roofing repair. One left open through a second storm cell often becomes a ceiling, insulation and drywall replacement too, because Cranberry's humid summer air lets drying times stretch and secondary damage spread.
- **Category of water.** Rain intrusion is usually Category 1 (clean) water if addressed quickly. If it sits long enough to pick up contaminants from attic insulation or ceiling material, it can shift toward Category 2, which changes what materials can be dried versus must be removed, per the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) that governs professional water drying.
- **Square footage affected.** A single second-floor bedroom ceiling is a different job than water tracking down through two floors into a finished basement, which is the common path in Cranberry's many two-story plans with second-floor baths and laundries.
- **Structural vs. cosmetic damage.** A missing shingle patch is cheap. A cracked roof truss or compromised decking from a fallen limb requires an engineer's sign-off before reconstruction, which adds time and cost.
- **Code upgrades on reconstruction.** Replacing a damaged roof section sometimes triggers current code requirements (ice-and-water shield, updated flashing details) that an older roof never had, which a contractor should flag before work begins.

For the water-mitigation piece specifically, costs track closely with general water damage restoration pricing; see our [PA water damage cost breakdown](/blog/water-damage-restoration-cost-pa/) for how extraction and drying are typically scoped.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Storm Damage Restoration?

Most standard homeowners policies cover wind and hail damage, including the water intrusion that follows a storm-created opening, but ground flooding is a separate, excluded peril that requires its own flood policy.

That distinction matters because it changes which deductible applies. Many PA homeowners policies carry a percentage-based wind/hail deductible, often 1% to 5% of the dwelling coverage limit, instead of (or in addition to) the flat dollar deductible that applies to other claims. On a $350,000 dwelling limit, a 2% wind deductible is $7,000 out of pocket before coverage kicks in, a very different number than a typical $1,000 flat deductible. Read your declarations page before the adjuster visit so there are no surprises. The [Insurance Information Institute's guide to what standard homeowners insurance covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) and its overview of [which disasters homeowners insurance covers](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) are good plain-language starting points.

If the damage involves rising creek water, such as Brush Creek backing up during a cloudburst, that's a flood claim, not a wind claim, and it falls under a [National Flood Insurance Program policy](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) rather than your homeowners policy. Our [storm damage insurance claim checklist](/blog/storm-damage-insurance-claim-checklist/) walks through documentation before you call the adjuster, which is worth doing before any cleanup starts.

## What Happens During a Storm Damage Restoration Job?

A storm damage job moves through four phases in order: stabilize the opening, remove the water, document for the claim, then rebuild.

1. **Emergency tarping or board-up.** This stops the bleeding, literally, by closing the roof or window opening so no more water or weather gets in. It usually happens same-day because every hour of delay adds to the water extraction phase.
2. **Water extraction and structural drying.** Wet insulation, drywall and flooring get extracted and dried with air movers and dehumidifiers, with moisture readings tracked daily until materials hit dry standard.
3. **Debris and tree coordination.** If a tree or large limb is involved, the restoration contractor typically coordinates with a tree removal service and documents the damage for the adjuster before anything gets hauled away; removing debris before it's photographed can complicate a claim.
4. **Reconstruction.** Roofing, drywall, insulation, flooring and trim get rebuilt to match the pre-loss condition, ideally from a written scope both you and your insurer have agreed to.
