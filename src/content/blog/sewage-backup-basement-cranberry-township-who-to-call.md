Before you make any calls, stay out of the water. Category 3 water, the technical term for sewage and any backup from a drain or sewer line, carries bacteria, viruses and parasites that can make you sick through a cut, a splash to the eyes, or contaminated air. Keep kids and pets out of the basement. Do not run a furnace, window fan, or dehumidifier over standing sewage; that spreads contaminated aerosols through the rest of the house instead of containing them.

## Do you call a plumber or a restoration company first?

Call a plumber or the municipal sewer authority first, while the water is still backing up: someone has to stop the source before anyone can clean up what it left behind. Call a restoration company once the flow has stopped, for the extraction, demolition, and sanitizing.

How you tell them apart matters. A backup from one isolated fixture, like only the basement floor drain or a utility sink gurgling, usually points to a clog in your own lateral line, and a licensed plumber with a drain snake or hydro-jetter can typically clear it. Older homes in Fernway and around Cranberry Heights still run original clay or cast-iron laterals that are prone to root intrusion and collapse, which is why those blocks see more lateral clogs than newer construction near Seven Fields or Ehrman Farms. If multiple fixtures across the house back up at once, or neighbors on the same street report the same problem, the blockage is more likely in the public main, and Cranberry Township's sewer department needs to clear it before anything inside the house gets fixed.

If the backup has created an electrical hazard, such as standing water near an outlet, a sump pump panel, or a furnace, shut off power to that area at the breaker only if you can reach it safely, and call an electrician or 911 before anyone goes back into the space.

## What happens when a restoration crew comes out for a sewage backup?

A restoration crew extracts the contaminated water, removes the porous materials that touched it, like carpet, pad and drywall, and treats what remains with an antimicrobial before drying starts. On arrival, a tech documents the affected area with photos for your insurer, sets containment to keep contaminated water from tracking into the rest of the house, and pumps or extracts standing water. ## Does homeowners insurance cover a sewer backup?

Usually not, unless you've added a water backup or sewer/drain endorsement to your policy. A standard homeowners policy typically excludes damage from a sewer or drain backup, so check your declarations page for that rider before you assume the claim is covered.

If the endorsement is there, document everything before cleanup starts: photos of the standing water, the affected rooms, and any damaged contents. You have a duty to mitigate further damage, so starting extraction promptly protects the claim rather than hurting it. [The Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) breaks down what a standard policy does and doesn't cover; our own [plain-English guide to homeowners insurance and water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) covers the claim process in more detail. A sewer backup and a flood are treated differently for insurance purposes too: flood water rising from outside the home needs a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/), while a backup originating inside your own plumbing needs the sewer/water backup endorsement instead.

## What can usually be saved after a sewage backup?

Hard, nonporous surfaces like tile, sealed concrete and metal fixtures can generally be cleaned and disinfected and kept. Porous materials that absorbed Category 3 water, like carpet, pad, drywall, insulation and most upholstered furniture, are typically removed and discarded rather than dried, per [IICRC S500](https://iicrc.org/s500/) guidance, because they hold contamination deep in their fibers.
