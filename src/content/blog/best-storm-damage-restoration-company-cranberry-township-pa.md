## What actually happens after a storm damages a Cranberry Township home?

A proper storm response starts with stopping the active intrusion, then moves into documenting and drying before anything gets rebuilt. The sequence matters because skipping a step is how homeowners end up with mold claims denied months later.

1. **Stabilize the opening.** Plywood over a broken window, a tarp over a torn section of roof, or sandbagging a basement entry point. This is the same emergency board-up and tarping work that stops the bleeding before anything else can happen.
2. **Extract standing water.** Pumps and extraction wands pull out bulk water from carpet, hardwood, and finished basements, the kind of flooding Cranberry's clay soil and Brush Creek drainage make common during cloudburst storms.
3. **Document the loss.** Photos of the damage, moisture readings on affected walls, and a written scope before demolition starts. This record is what your insurer actually reads when deciding what's covered.
4. **Dry the structure.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture meter readings on studs and subfloor return to normal, not just until the carpet looks dry on the surface.
5. **Rebuild.** Drywall, flooring, and trim go back once the structure tests dry, matching what was there before rather than leaving a half-finished patch.

That sequence is the same whether the trigger was wind damage to a roof over Fox Run or a storm drain backup near the Cranberry Township Community Park. The storm breaks something open; what happens in the next 48 hours decides whether it stays a repair or becomes a mold remediation job too.

## How do you spot a storm-chaser contractor before you sign anything?

A storm chaser is an out-of-area crew that follows severe weather into a region, knocks on doors offering fast tarping, and disappears once paid, often with no local presence to come back to. The giveaways are consistent: an out-of-state license plate, no permanent local address, pressure to sign a contract on the spot, and a demand for payment in full before any work starts.

Pennsylvania requires home improvement contractors to register with the Office of Attorney General under the state's [Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/protect-yourself/home-improvement/). You can look up any contractor's registration number before you let them on your roof. If someone can't give you a registration number, or the number doesn't come back valid, that's your answer. FireDEX Butler's registration is PA001913; our guide on [choosing a restoration company](/blog/choosing-a-restoration-company/) covers the rest of the vetting checklist in more detail, including what a legitimate estimate should look like.

A few more flags worth checking before you sign: does the company have a physical address you can find independently of their truck's magnetic sign, and will they put the scope of work in writing before taking a deposit? A legitimate local contractor has no reason to avoid either question.

## Why is storm damage really a water damage emergency?

Most storm damage claims in Cranberry Township start as wind or hail damage and turn into water damage within hours, because a breached roof or broken window doesn't stop the rain, it just lets more of it in. The structural damage from the storm itself is usually the smaller part of the loss; the water that follows is what causes the mold, warped flooring, and drywall replacement that drive up the claim.

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold) notes that mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, which is why the tarp-then-dry sequence above isn't optional paperwork, it's the thing that keeps a roof repair from becoming a full remediation job. Our post on [how quickly mold grows after water damage](/blog/mold-after-water-damage/) goes deeper on that timeline if you want to understand what you're racing against.

This is also why a water-focused restoration company tends to handle storm claims better than a roofing-only contractor: the roof is the entry point, but the water damage underneath is usually the larger insurance line item, and it needs the same moisture-tracking and drying process as any other flood.
