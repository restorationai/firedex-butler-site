**TL;DR:** After an unattended death, the home is not yours to clean until local police and the Butler County Coroner's office finish examining the scene and release it. Families should not attempt the cleanup themselves because of bloodborne pathogen risk and materials that hold odor and contamination long after the obvious cleanup looks done. A professional biohazard and trauma cleanup company handles decontamination, odor removal, and safe disposal of affected materials, then documents the loss so it can be submitted to insurance.

If someone passed away alone in a Cranberry Township home, whether in Fernway, Fox Run, or anywhere else in the township, and was not found for some time, you are facing two separate questions right now: who has legal authority over the scene, and who can safely clean it once that authority releases it. Those two questions have different answers, and getting the order wrong can slow down both the cleanup and any insurance claim that follows.

## Who do you call first after an unattended death?

The first call is 911, not a cleanup company. Local police and the Butler County Coroner's office have jurisdiction over the scene until their examination is complete, and nobody, including family, should enter or disturb the area before that happens.

The coroner's office determines cause and manner of death and will release the scene once their work is finished, sometimes the same day, sometimes longer depending on the circumstances. Only after that release does the property legally become something a family or a cleanup crew can enter and work on.

## Should the family clean up the scene themselves?

No. Bodily fluids and the materials they soak into carry a real risk of bloodborne pathogens, and that risk does not go away once the visible part looks cleaned.

Flooring, subfloor, drywall, and insulation are porous. They absorb fluids below the surface, which means a mop and a bottle of bleach address what is visible while leaving contamination, and the odor that comes with it, in place. The [OSHA bloodborne pathogens standard](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens) exists specifically because this kind of exposure is a recognized occupational hazard, which is the same reason it is not a reasonable task for a grieving family member to take on. Professional cleanup follows practices outlined in the [IICRC S540 standard](https://iicrc.org/s540/) for trauma and crime scene cleanup, which covers decontamination, safe disposal, and verification that the area is clean before anyone moves back in.

## What does professional cleanup involve, and how long does it take?

Professional cleanup removes and disposes of contaminated materials, decontaminates the surrounding area, and treats the space for odor before anyone considers it move-in ready. The scope depends entirely on how long the scene went unattended and what materials were affected.

A crew typically starts by identifying everything affected, flooring, drywall, subfloor, personal items, and removing what cannot be safely cleaned. The remaining surfaces are disinfected, and odor from decomposition, which can be the most persistent part of the job, is addressed separately with equipment like hydroxyl generators or thermal fogging rather than masked with air freshener. A single affected room typically takes one to three days of active work, though a longer unattended period or an older home with original subflooring can extend that. Odor is often the last thing to fully resolve, which is why it gets its own [odor removal](/services/odor-removal/) step rather than being treated as an afterthought of the [biohazard cleanup](/services/biohazard-cleanup/) itself.

Families researching cost ahead of time can find typical ranges in [our Cranberry Township biohazard cleanup cost guide](/blog/biohazard-cleanup-cost-cranberry-township/), though every scene is different and a written scope should come before any work begins.

## Does homeowners insurance cover unattended death cleanup?

Many standard homeowners policies include some coverage for biohazard or trauma cleanup, but the amount and the triggering conditions vary by insurer and policy, so this is not something to assume. Triple-I's overview of [what is covered by standard homeowners insurance](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) is a reasonable starting point for understanding how insurers generally categorize these losses, but your specific policy language is what actually governs your claim.

A restoration company that documents the scope of work, the materials removed, and the before-and-after condition of the property gives the claim a much better chance of moving smoothly. That documentation is also something a family should not have to produce themselves in the middle of a loss like this.
