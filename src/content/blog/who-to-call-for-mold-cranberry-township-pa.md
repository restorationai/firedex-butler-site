**TL;DR:** Small, isolated mold spots under about 10 square feet, roughly a dinner-plate-to-card-table-sized patch, can usually be cleaned with detergent and a scrub brush per EPA guidance. Mold spreading across framing, growing back after cleaning, tied to a water loss, or flagged during a home inspection calls for a mold remediation company that fixes the moisture source first, then removes and cleans the affected materials. Lab testing helps confirm scope or species but isn't required to start a cleanup.

Maybe you pulled a dresser away from a basement wall in Fernway after this spring's sump pump scare and found a gray-green patch creeping up the drywall. Or a home inspector in Park Place flagged "elevated moisture and suspected mold" during a sale, and now the closing is on hold. Either way, the question is the same: can you handle this with bleach and a sponge, or do you need to call someone. The size of the patch and what's feeding it answer that, not how alarming it looks.

## Can I clean a small mold spot myself?

Yes. The [EPA says](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) homeowners can clean mold on hard, non-porous surfaces smaller than about 10 square feet, roughly a 3-foot by 3-foot area, with household detergent, water and a stiff brush, as long as you wear gloves, an N95 mask and eye protection.

The catch is the material underneath. Drywall paper, carpet, ceiling tile and insulation are porous: mold grows into them, not just on them, so scrubbing the surface doesn't remove it. Porous material with visible mold usually needs to be cut out and bagged, not cleaned. And none of this matters if the surface gets wet again in a week, which is why the next question comes before any scrubbing starts.

## When does mold become a remediation job instead of a weekend project?

Mold stops being a DIY fix once the affected area exceeds roughly 10 square feet, sits inside a wall cavity or HVAC duct, keeps coming back after you've cleaned it, or traces back to a water event like a sump pump failure or a slow foundation seep.

Cranberry Township's basement-heavy housing stock makes that last trigger common. Clay soil pushes hydrostatic pressure through foundation walls in wet springs, and a lot of Fernway-era homes still run original plumbing that fails quietly before anyone notices. If mold shows up after [a basement leak](/blog/mold-after-water-damage/) or you're seeing [other signs of hidden mold](/blog/signs-of-hidden-mold/) behind trim or under flooring, the visible patch is probably smaller than the actual problem.

Health complaints from anyone in the house, mold found during a real estate inspection, or mold in a finished basement where contents and framing are both at risk are also reasons to bring in a professional rather than guess.

## Why does the moisture source get fixed before anyone touches the mold?

Because mold can start regrowing within 24 to 48 hours on a wet surface, so cleaning or removing it without fixing what made it wet is a temporary, cosmetic fix.

A proper remediation visit starts by finding the source, whether that's a failed sump pump, a slow supply-line drip behind a wall, or hydrostatic seepage through a foundation crack, and either repairing it or confirming it's already fixed. Only then does the crew contain the work area with poly sheeting, remove and bag affected porous materials, HEPA-vacuum and clean the remaining surfaces, and run air scrubbers to control airborne spores before anything gets rebuilt. If the leak is still active or hard to pin down, [water leak detection](/services/water-leak-detection/) usually happens first.

## Do I need mold testing before remediation starts?

Not always. Visible mold with an identified, now-fixed source usually doesn't need a lab test before cleanup begins; the EPA notes that if you can see or smell mold, testing generally isn't necessary to know it needs to be removed.

Testing earns its cost when you need documentation, for a home sale disclosure, a post-remediation clearance report, or an insurance file, or when you suspect mold inside ductwork or a wall cavity with no visible growth yet. Air sampling and surface swabs can confirm that, and a [mold inspection](/services/mold-inspection-testing/) is also the right call if you're just not sure what you're looking at. For a side-by-side on kits versus a pro inspection, see [how to test for mold](/blog/how-to-test-for-mold/).
