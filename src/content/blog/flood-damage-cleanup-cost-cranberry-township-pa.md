## Is flood water the same as the water from a burst pipe?

No. Flood water and a burst supply line are different categories under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), and the category changes both the cleanup cost and what gets thrown out.

A burst pipe is usually Category 1, clean water, at least at first. Groundwater that seeps through your foundation walls under hydrostatic pressure, the kind of seepage common in Cranberry Township's clay soil during a wet spring, is Category 2, significantly contaminated water, because it's picked up soil and groundwater contaminants on the way in.

Water that rises from Brush Creek, or surface runoff that flows in across the yard and through a window well, is Category 3, black water. That's the same classification as sewage. Category 3 water requires removing porous materials like carpet, carpet pad, and often drywall up to the waterline, rather than drying them in place, which is why creek-overflow and surface-runoff jobs usually cost more than a seepage job of the same size. ## Does homeowners insurance cover flood damage, or do I need NFIP flood insurance?

A standard homeowners policy typically does not cover flood damage: that includes water from an overflowing creek, surface runoff, or rising groundwater. Coverage for those sources requires a separate flood insurance policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) or a private flood carrier.

This catches a lot of Cranberry Township homeowners off guard, because the same basement can flood from two different sources in the same year: a burst pipe (usually covered under a standard policy as sudden, accidental water damage) and a creek overflow or heavy-rain seepage event (usually excluded, and requiring NFIP or private flood coverage). The [Insurance Information Institute's guide to disaster coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) breaks this distinction down in plain terms. We go deeper on the standard-policy side in [Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage?](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/)

If you do carry NFIP coverage, document everything before cleanup starts. Photos of the water line, a list of affected contents, and a written scope of work support the claim and your duty to mitigate further damage.

## What drives the final bill up or down?

The biggest cost driver is how long the water sat before extraction started, because standing water keeps spreading contamination and saturating materials the longer it's there. A basement pumped out within a few hours almost always costs less than one discovered two days later.

Other factors that move the number:

- **Water category.** Category 1 jobs dry in place; Category 2 and 3 jobs require removing porous materials, which adds demo and disposal cost.
- **Square footage and ceiling height.** More air volume means more air movers and dehumidifiers running longer.
- **What's affected.** Drywall and insulation are cheaper to replace than hardwood flooring, finished millwork, or a built-in wet bar, which is common in Wexford and Seven Fields walk-out basements.
- **Contents.** Furniture, electronics, and stored items that need cleaning, pack-out, or disposal add a separate line item.
- **Code upgrades.** If the rebuild triggers current code (GFCI outlets, updated insulation), that's an additional cost beyond matching what was there.

## What actually happens during flood damage cleanup?

Flood cleanup follows a sequence: assess and stop the water source, extract standing water, remove contaminated materials if needed, dry the structure, then rebuild. Skipping steps to save money usually costs more later in mold remediation.

1. **Safety check and water shutoff.** Confirm there's no electrical hazard, identify the water source, and stop active inflow where possible (sump backup, sandbag, grading fix).
2. **Extraction.** Pumps and truck-mounted extractors remove standing water, typically within the first few hours of arrival.
3. **Contaminated material removal.** For Category 2 or 3 water, carpet, pad, and affected drywall come out rather than being dried in place.
4. **Structural drying.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run for several days, with daily moisture readings to confirm materials are drying on schedule.
5. **Antimicrobial treatment.** Applied to framing and subfloor after contaminated water exposure, before any rebuild begins.
6. **Rebuild.** Drywall, insulation, trim, and flooring go back in to match the pre-loss condition.

A basement with clean, quickly-extracted water can be dry in three to five days.
