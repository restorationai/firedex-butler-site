## Who should I call first for mold remediation in Cranberry Township?

Call a licensed mold remediation contractor first if the growth covers more than roughly 10 square feet or keeps coming back, since that's past the do-it-yourself threshold the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home) sets for homeowner cleanup. A home improvement website or hardware-store bleach spray handles small, isolated spots. It does not handle mold behind drywall, under carpet pad, or inside HVAC ductwork.

If the source is still wet, active plumbing or an ongoing roof leak, that needs to be shut off before remediation starts; otherwise the mold just comes back in the same spot within a few weeks. A plumber or roofer stops the water. A remediation crew removes the contaminated material, contains the area with poly sheeting, and runs negative-air HEPA filtration while the work is done, following the [ANSI/IICRC S520 mold remediation standard](https://iicrc.org/s520/).

## How do I know if it's a DIY job or a professional one?

Size, location, and recurrence decide it: small, surface-level, non-recurring spots are usually fine to clean yourself; anything larger, hidden, or repeating needs a professional assessment. A single ring of mildew on a window sill after a humid week is a wipe-down job. Mold that reappears every spring behind the same baseboard, or that you can smell but can't see, usually means moisture is trapped inside a wall cavity or under flooring.

A mold inspection with a moisture meter and, if needed, air or surface sampling settles the question before anyone starts tearing out drywall. That matters in older Fernway and Marshall Heights homes with galvanized plumbing, where a slow pinhole leak inside a wall can feed mold growth for months before it's visible. ## What happens when a mold remediation company arrives?

The first visit is an assessment: moisture readings, a visual inspection of the affected materials, and a scope of work before anything is removed. The crew identifies how far the contamination has spread behind visible surfaces, confirms whether the moisture source has been resolved, and documents everything in photos for your insurance file.

Once the scope is set, the area gets sealed off with plastic sheeting and run under negative air pressure so spores don't spread to the rest of the house. Porous materials that can't be cleaned, such as soaked drywall or carpet pad, get removed and bagged. Hard surfaces get HEPA-vacuumed and treated with an antimicrobial, then the space is dried and re-tested to confirm the job is complete before the area is cleared to rebuild.

## Does homeowners insurance cover mold remediation?

Usually only when the mold resulted from a sudden, covered loss, like a burst pipe, not from a slow leak or long-term humidity. [Standard homeowners policies](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) generally cap mold coverage or exclude it outright when the cause is gradual seepage, condensation, or deferred maintenance. If the mold followed a sudden pipe burst that you reported and mitigated promptly, there's a reasonable chance it's covered under the water damage portion of your claim. Mold that followed a sump pump failure is typically covered only if your policy includes a separate water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement, since a standard policy usually excludes sump pump failures. If it's been building for months behind a wall you only just opened up, expect a harder conversation with the adjuster.

Documentation helps either way. A written inspection report, moisture readings, and dated photos from a mold inspection are the kind of evidence an adjuster wants to see, regardless of which way the claim goes.
