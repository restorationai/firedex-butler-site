**TL;DR:** When your basement floods, do three things before anything else: cut power to the basement at the breaker, shut off the gas if you smell it, and identify where the water came from. If it entered through a floor drain or toilet backup, treat it as sewage contamination and stay out until a pro arrives. If it's clean water from a burst pipe or sump failure, you can begin extracting once the electricity is confirmed off. Mold can start growing within 24 to 48 hours, so the clock matters from the moment you discover the water.

You walked downstairs and found water. Maybe it's an inch, maybe it's a foot. Maybe the sump pit is overflowing, or the floor drain is bubbling up, or you can hear water still running somewhere overhead. Whatever the source, the next hour is the most important one. This guide covers the basement-specific steps that most general water damage guides skip: the electrical and gas shutoff sequence, how to read your sump pit and floor drain, and the decisions that are different when the basement is finished.

## Is It Safe To Enter a Flooded Basement?

Do not step into standing water in your basement until you have confirmed the electricity is off. Water and live circuits are a lethal combination, and basement outlets, sump pump wiring, and HVAC equipment are often close to floor level. Go to your main electrical panel first and switch off every breaker that feeds the basement. If the panel itself is in the basement and is near the water, do not enter to reach it. Call your utility company or a licensed electrician to disconnect power from outside.

Once power is confirmed off, check for gas. If you smell anything like rotten eggs, leave the house immediately, leave the door open, and call your gas company from outside. Do not flip any switches or use your phone inside the house if you suspect a gas leak.

After power and gas are handled, identify the water source before you start removing anything. That source determines whether the water is safe to touch.

## What Kind of Water Is in Your Basement?

The [IICRC S500 standard](https://www.iicrc.org/page/IICRCS500) classifies water damage into three categories, and the category changes everything about how cleanup proceeds.

**Category 1 (clean water):** Came from a supply line, a water heater, or rainfall entering through a window well. Safe to handle with basic gloves. This is the most common source in homes with a sump pump failure during a heavy storm.

**Category 2 (gray water):** Came from a washing machine drain, dishwasher, or a sump pit that has been sitting. Contains mild contaminants. Wear gloves and avoid contact with eyes or open cuts.

**Category 3 (black water):** Came from a floor drain backing up, a sewage ejector pump failure, or an overflowing toilet. This water contains raw sewage and is a serious health hazard. Do not enter without full PPE, and in most cases you should wait for a professional [sewage cleanup](/services/sewage-cleanup/) crew before doing anything beyond shutting off the source.

If you are not sure which category applies, assume the worst. A floor drain that is bubbling during a heavy rain is almost certainly Category 3 because municipal sewer systems back up into floor drains when they are overwhelmed. This is one of the most common basement flood scenarios in Butler County during summer cloudburst storms, and it is not a DIY cleanup situation.

## How To Stop More Water From Entering

Before you extract a single gallon, stop the inflow. Pumping out water while more is coming in is wasted effort.

1. **Burst or leaking supply line:** Turn off the main water shutoff to the house. If you have a burst pipe specifically, see the [burst pipe repair guide](/services/burst-pipe-repair/) for the shutoff sequence.
2. **Sump pump failure:** Check whether the pump lost power (breaker tripped, outlet GFCI tripped) or whether the pump itself failed. A tripped GFCI outlet is a two-second fix. A dead pump during an active storm means you need a backup pump or a wet vac running continuously until the water table drops.
3. **Floor drain backup:** You cannot stop this from inside. The pressure is coming from the municipal sewer or the drain tile system. Get a licensed plumber or restoration crew on the phone.
4. **Hydrostatic seepage through foundation walls:** This is groundwater pushing through cracks or the cove joint at the base of the wall. It will slow on its own once the storm passes and the soil drains, but it will not stop until the water table drops. Extraction is the only option while it is active.
5. **Window well overflow:** Clear debris from the window well drain if it is clogged. If there is no drain, a wet vac at the window well can reduce pressure while you address the interior.

## How To Extract the Water

Once the source is stopped and electricity is confirmed off, start removing water as fast as possible. Speed matters: the [FEMA flood safety guidance](https://www.ready.gov/floods) notes that standing water in a structure creates immediate health and structural risks that compound with time.

For less than two inches of water, a wet/dry shop vac works but is slow. A submersible sump pump dropped into the deepest point of the basement and routed to a floor drain (a working one) or a garden hose run outside is much faster. For more than two inches, a gas-powered trash pump or a professional truck-mounted extractor will cut hours off the job.

A few basement-specific extraction notes:
- **Check the sump pit.** If the pit is full, the pump float may be stuck. Manually lift the float to test the pump before assuming it is dead.
- **Do not pump water into the floor drain if it was the source of backup.** You will be fighting yourself. Route extraction discharge outside or to a utility sink on a different drain line.
- **Carpet over concrete:** Pull back a corner and check the pad. Carpet dries slowly; carpet pad almost never dries in place. If the pad is saturated, it needs to come out. Leaving it traps moisture against the concrete slab and accelerates mold growth.

## Finished Basement: The Decisions That Are Different

An unfinished basement with exposed concrete walls and a bare slab is straightforward to dry. A finished basement with drywall, carpet, insulation, and built-in cabinetry is a different problem.

**Drywall:** Standard drywall wicks water up by capillary action. Even if the flood was only two inches deep, the drywall may be wet 12 to 18 inches above the waterline. Wet drywall that is not removed or aggressively dried will grow mold inside the wall cavity within 48 to 72 hours. The standard approach is to cut a flood cut: remove drywall to 12 inches above the visible waterline to expose the framing and insulation for drying. This is a controlled demolition decision, not a guess.

**Insulation:** Fiberglass batt insulation that gets wet loses its R-value and holds moisture against the framing. It almost always needs to be removed and replaced. Spray foam insulation is more water-resistant and may survive if dried quickly.

**Carpet and pad:** As noted above, the pad goes. The carpet itself can sometimes be saved if it is Category 1 water, extracted immediately, and dried within 24 hours with proper airflow. Category 2 or 3 water means the carpet comes out.

**Cabinetry and built-ins:** Particleboard and MDF swell when wet and do not recover. Solid wood and plywood are more forgiving if dried quickly. Document everything with photos before moving or removing anything. Your insurance adjuster will need that documentation. ## How Long Does It Take To Dry Out a Flooded Basement?

A typical basement flood with clean water takes 3 to 5 days to dry to acceptable moisture levels using professional equipment: commercial LGR dehumidifiers, high-velocity air movers, and daily moisture readings. An unfinished basement with no materials to replace may be on the shorter end. A finished basement with drywall, framing, and subfloor materials will take longer, especially if the concrete slab is thick.

Do not judge dryness by feel or by whether the floor looks dry. Concrete holds moisture long after the surface appears dry. A calibrated moisture meter is the only reliable way to confirm that structural materials have reached an acceptable moisture content before reconstruction begins. Rushing this step is the most common reason mold appears weeks after a flood. For a deeper look at timelines, see [how long water damage restoration actually takes](/blog/how-long-does-water-damage-restoration-take/).

## What To Do Next

If the flood was small, the water was clean, and the basement is unfinished, a careful DIY extraction and drying effort with rented equipment is reasonable. If any of the following are true, call a restoration professional before you do more than extract standing water:

- The water came from a floor drain or sewage source
- The basement is finished with drywall, carpet, or insulation
- You cannot confirm the water source
- The water has been sitting for more than a few hours
- You see or smell anything that suggests mold is already present
