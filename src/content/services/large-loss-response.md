**Large loss and catastrophic response** covers the losses that don't fit inside a normal mitigation timeline: a collapsed roof section that floods three floors of a commercial building, a burst main that fills an entire basement and first floor overnight, a fire that takes out a building's electrical and HVAC systems along with the structure, or a multi-unit property where water or smoke has traveled through shared walls and corridors. These jobs need more crews, more equipment, and a different kind of project management than a single-room water loss. If you're staring at a property with damage spread across multiple floors, units, or buildings, you're already past the point where a standard service call solves the problem.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A large loss is defined less by the dollar figure and more by scale and complexity: multiple affected structures or floors, damage that's spreading faster than a small crew can contain, or a commercial operation that needs to stay partially functional while restoration happens around it. That might mean a manufacturing facility with water intrusion near production lines, a multi-building apartment complex after a storm, or a retail space with fire and smoke damage that's also a liability and access problem. These jobs typically pull in extraction trucks, commercial-grade air movers and dehumidifiers by the dozen, temporary power and containment barriers, and a project lead who's coordinating subcontractors, property management, and the insurance carrier's large loss team at the same time. Timelines run from days to months depending on the structure and the extent of reconstruction needed afterward.

## Our process

1. **Rapid mobilization and triage.** Crews arrive and assess which areas are actively deteriorating versus stable, so resources go where they stop the most damage first.
2. **Structural and systems assessment.** We document affected square footage, building systems (electrical, HVAC, plumbing), and life-safety concerns before crews start moving equipment or materials.
3. **Multi-phase mitigation.** Extraction, demolition of unsalvageable materials, and drying happen in zones rather than all at once, with moisture readings logged daily per zone so nothing gets signed off early.
4. **Carrier coordination and documentation.** Scope, photos, moisture logs, and line-item estimates go to the adjuster's large loss desk in the format they expect, which keeps approvals from stalling the job.
5. **Reconstruction handoff.** Once a space is dry and clean, it moves into our reconstruction scope, whether that's a single damaged wing or a full rebuild, with permitting handled as part of the scope.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The biggest failure point on large losses isn't the drying equipment, it's coordination. Crews that treat a 40,000 square foot loss like a bigger version of a residential job tend to under-resource it: not enough air movers for the actual cubic footage, moisture logs that cover "the building" instead of each affected zone, and no clear phasing plan, which means some areas sit wet for days longer than they should. Adjusters on large commercial claims are looking for zone-by-zone documentation, not a single summary report, and they want to see a written scope before demolition starts, not after. Another common miss is ignoring business interruption: if part of a facility can be dried and cleared faster than the rest, phasing the work so that section reopens sooner matters as much as the technical drying itself. Good large loss response also means having a single point of contact managing subcontractors and the carrier relationship, instead of the property manager fielding five different crew leads with five different answers.

## What does large loss and catastrophic response cost?

Cost on large and catastrophic losses depends heavily on square footage, how many trades are involved, and whether reconstruction is bundled into the scope. Every loss is different, which is why we provide a written scope of work before any demolition or mitigation begins rather than a flat quote up front. Commercial property insurance typically covers large loss mitigation and reconstruction, often with a dedicated large loss adjuster assigned to the claim, though business interruption and certain equipment or inventory losses may fall under separate policy sections.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Western Pennsylvania's winter freeze-thaw cycles are a common trigger for large commercial losses, a frozen sprinkler line or rooftop unit failing in a vacant or lightly staffed building can run undetected for hours and flood multiple floors before anyone notices. Spring thaw and heavy regional rainfall add a second peak for storm-driven large losses, particularly for properties with flat or low-slope commercial roofing where ponding and drainage failures compound quickly. Buildings with aging infrastructure around Cranberry Township and the surrounding Butler County commercial corridors are more prone to the kind of cascading systems failures that turn a single pipe break into a true large loss.
